## Building skills for SDK docs

For technical writers without a development background, documenting a software development kit (SDK) can be a big ask. A question this month asked how SDK documentarians build their skills. The original poster felt that API documentation can be a transferable skill: once you understand how an API request is structured, you can generally apply this knowledge to document any API. SDKs are different. Each one is tied to a programming language, so documenting it well could take some proficiency in that language.

The reassuring news from the thread is that you may need less programming expertise than you think. One contributor who has used many programming languages professionally said, "After a while they all begin to look a little same-y because, paradigmatically, they are." Another contributor, who works on many different SDKs, found that domain and product knowledge mattered more than knowing the language, especially when rewriting the docs for new users and LLMs.

Contributors shared some practical ways to build skills for documenting SDKs and get support:

* **Read up on computer science**. Skim books from an undergraduate computer science reading list to build your vocabulary. "You don’t have to read them cover to cover, but a thorough skimming and trying some of the exercises can be really fruitful," suggested one contributor.  
* **Build something real**. Using a language hands-on puts you in the position of the people reading your docs. A Raspberry Pi project was suggested as a low-friction, fun place to start.  
* **Lean on your engineering team**. They can help when you’re out of your depth.  
* **Use AI tools**. LLMs can help when you’re documenting languages you’re less familiar with.

One documentarian described building her knowledge gradually: starting with one SDK, moving on to the SDK for a similar language, and going from there. By the end of that role, she was writing SDK docs in 12 languages. As she put it: "For me, it was starting with one and getting really solid, and then using a progression that made sense for me to branch out, instead of trying to do all of it from the beginning."

## To include screenshots or not

A writer was asked recently to include annotated screenshots with every step in procedures. The posting prompted a discussion about when one should or shouldn’t include screenshots.

**When to include**

Use screenshots when they help users understand the UI or complete a task more effectively.

* When orienting themselves in an unfamiliar UI, some users find images in documentation to be beneficial.
* A screenshot can help users identify elements in a complex UI with hidden features. 
* At the end of a complex procedure or configuration, a screenshot may clarify an expected result or required settings. 
* A relevant screenshot can draw attention to specific areas of the UI.
* If your product UI has similar elements, screenshots can help users distinguish between them.

**When to not include**

Avoid screenshots when they add little value, require significant ongoing maintenance, or create accessibility problems.

* Do not use screenshots with simple instructions that can be explained clearly with text.
* Avoid screenshots of frequently updated UIs. (Inaccurate screenshots cause confusion and, often, support tickets and lost sales.)
* Avoid screenshots that have an unclear visual focus.
* Do not include screenshots just to make content more visually appealing.
* Avoid screenshots as the sole source of information. Many users (including chatbots) access instructions and essential information through text.
* Many users (including chatbots) search for content, so focus on textual content that’s searchable. 
* If your program is localized, localizing screenshots can be problematic.

Most documentarians try to minimize the number of screenshots used. Many suggested working with the UI/UX team and developers to make the UI easy to understand without needing screenshots. If you can’t influence changes to the UI, gather feedback from users (perhaps A/B test documentation with and without screenshots) to determine your users' preferences. 

The goal is *not* to maximize the number of screenshots, but to provide the right visual support at the right time for users to accomplish their tasks. Documentarians mention removing many screenshots from existing documentation and never getting negative feedback.

## Do writing skills help you code better?

A recent discussion in [#ai](https://writethedocs.slack.com/archives/C1NEAD7D4) addressed the prevalence of AI tools in both developing code and documentation, leading some to wonder whether writing ability can enhance one’s output in a programming/software engineering context. One contributor highlighted that documentarians know how to write, which is a key differentiator in today’s market, where much development is done through prose rather than code. If agentic models are doing the implementation, how do you ensure that solutions are implemented correctly and failures are identified? Strong systems thinking and a high-level view are essential.

The relationship between writing prowess and coding ability was also examined through the lens of what attributes correlate most effectively with successful AI usage. One member viewed presenting ideas and crafting a clear vision as more important than writing skills. What other assets are essential to optimizing one’s coding performance besides writing? It’s possible to possess strong domain knowledge in one field but not have enough of a technical background to code something without the assistance of an LLM. That understanding, coupled with research skills, product-management instincts, clarity of vision, and an ability to distill key knowledge from disparate parts, distinguishes documentarians who can see one line "above the code" from those who merely write without building an understanding of what they write about.

People noted that the practice of writing itself encourages a clarity of thought that is worth acknowledging in the context of leveraging agentic models efficiently. As documentarians, you may possess certain critical reasoning and thinking traits that are taken for granted because they go hand in hand with habitual writing. Your soft skills might go overlooked because they’re ingrained. These adjacent skills that accompany quality writing are not easily measured through traditional metrics, but may be demonstrated through presenting clear systems-level thinking to a wide audience.

Respondents agreed that documentarians and engineers have a common ground: both know how to translate between human and machine and how to elucidate the vague and opaque into something easy to understand. Historically, the first programmers were writers and mathematicians, which showcases the common roots these professions share.
